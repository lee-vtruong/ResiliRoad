\begin{abstract}
We estimate structural connectivity loss in spatial graphs under simultaneous
edge disruptions.  We compare a first-order Fiedler-vector approximation, a
direct graph convolutional network (GCN), an ablation without the node-level
Fiedler feature, and a residual spectral--GCN that learns a bounded correction
to the analytical estimate.  A graph-disjoint evaluation uses 10,000 synthetic
scenarios over five seeds, followed by zero-shot transfer to 1,500 scenarios on
a 163-node OpenStreetMap road network in Ho Chi Minh City.  On synthetic test
graphs, residual learning reduces mean absolute error from
$0.0656\pm0.0132$ to $0.0390\pm0.0099$ and raises Spearman correlation from
$0.6279\pm0.0571$ to $0.8764\pm0.0375$.  Removing the Fiedler node feature has
only a small effect, showing that the improvement arises mainly from using the
spectral estimate as a residual prior.  On OpenStreetMap transfer, the residual
model achieves $0.1018\pm0.0155$ MAE and $0.9269\pm0.0119$ Spearman
correlation.  The standalone first-order approximation fails under this sparse
network shift, demonstrating both the value and limitation of the hybrid
correction.  This is a structural-disruption study, not a flood-risk model.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Road-network disruptions caused by extreme weather, construction, or local
failures can alter global connectivity even when only a small subset of links
is unavailable.  A common computational task is therefore to rank many
possible disruption scenarios.  Exact spectral recomputation is well defined,
but a fast surrogate may become useful when the number of networks and
scenarios is large.

This work connects two viewpoints.  Spectral graph theory supplies a precise
connectivity measure and an interpretable local sensitivity.  Graph neural
networks (GNNs) provide a data-driven mechanism for representing interactions
among several failed edges.  Instead of presuming that the learned model must
dominate the mathematical approximation, we ask where each method is reliable.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item a normalized prediction target comparable across graphs;
  \item an explicit first-order Fiedler-vector baseline;
  \item a small graph-level regressor requiring only standard PyTorch;
  \item a graph-disjoint experimental split that prevents structural leakage;
  \item a five-seed comparison stratified by disruption size;
  \item a Fiedler-feature ablation, a residual hybrid, and zero-shot OSM validation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Mathematical formulation}

Let $G=(V,E,w)$ be a connected, undirected graph with positive edge weights.
Its weighted adjacency matrix is $A$, degree matrix is $D$, and combinatorial
Laplacian is $L=D-A$.  Write the ordered eigenvalues as
\[
  0=\lambda_1(L)\leq \lambda_2(L)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{|V|}(L).
\]
Fiedler~\cite{fiedler1973} introduced $\lambda_2$ as the algebraic
connectivity.  It is positive exactly when an undirected graph is connected.

\begin{defn}[Relative connectivity loss]
For a set of disrupted edges $S\subseteq E$, let $G-S$ denote the damaged
graph.  We define
\begin{equation}
 y(G,S)=\operatorname{clip}\left(
 1-\frac{\lambda_2(G-S)}{\lambda_2(G)},0,1\right).
 \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
Thus $y=0$ represents no measured loss, while $y=1$ includes disconnection.
\end{defn}

Suppose $\lambda_2$ is simple and $u_2$ is its unit eigenvector.  For edge
$e=(i,j)$, standard symmetric eigenvalue perturbation gives
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\partial\lambda_2}{\partial w_e}=(u_{2,i}-u_{2,j})^2.
 \label{eq:sensitivity}
\end{equation}
Removing several edges is a finite, not infinitesimal, perturbation.  We use
the clipped first-order prediction
\begin{equation}
 \widehat y_{\mathrm{spec}}(G,S)=\operatorname{clip}\left(
 \frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in S}w_{ij}(u_{2,i}-u_{2,j})^2}{\lambda_2(G)},0,1\right).
 \label{eq:baseline}
\end{equation}
This expression ignores higher-order interaction and can be inaccurate near an
eigenvalue crossing or disconnection.  Its advantages are transparency and the
absence of learned parameters.

\section{ScenarioGCN}

For each scenario, the model receives the normalized adjacency matrix of
$G-S$, augmented with self-loops, and five node features: normalized degree in
$G$, fraction of incident edges in $S$, absolute normalized Fiedler coordinate,
and two spatial coordinates.  Three graph convolution layers use the update
\[
 H^{(\ell+1)}=\operatorname{ReLU}
 \left(\widetilde D^{-1/2}\widetilde A\widetilde D^{-1/2}
 H^{(\ell)}W^{(\ell)}\right),
\]
following the localized propagation principle of Kipf and
Welling~\cite{kipf2017}.  Mean and max graph pooling are concatenated with the
mean failed-edge incidence rate.  A two-layer head with sigmoid output predicts
Equation~\eqref{eq:target}.  The model has three 48-dimensional hidden layers,
dropout $0.15$, and is trained using AdamW and mean squared error.

We evaluate three learned configurations.  \emph{Direct-full} uses all five
features.  \emph{Direct-no-Fiedler} removes the third feature while preserving
the architecture and split.  The residual model predicts a bounded correction
$r_\theta$ to the analytical estimate:
\begin{equation}
 \widehat y_{\mathrm{res}}(G,S)=\operatorname{clip}
 \bigl(\widehat y_{\mathrm{spec}}(G,S)+\tanh r_\theta(G,S),0,1\bigr).
 \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
This distinguishes a Fiedler coordinate used as a node feature from the
analytical estimate used as an explicit inductive prior.

\section{Experimental design}

\subsection{Synthetic spatial graphs}
Random geometric graphs place nodes in the unit square and connect pairs
within a radius.  They are a basic spatial-network model rather than a faithful
road generator.  We adapt the radius until a connected graph with 35--65 nodes
is obtained.  Edge conductance is set to inverse Euclidean length.  For each
base graph we sample 20 scenarios, removing between one and eight edges.

The full benchmark contains five independent runs of 2,000 scenarios.  Within
each run, 70\%, 15\%, and 15\% of the scenarios are assigned to training,
validation, and test sets by \emph{base graph identifier}.  Consequently no
damaged version of a training graph appears in the test set.  Each run has
1,400 training, 300 validation, and 300 test scenarios, for a pooled total of
1,500 held-out predictions.

\subsection{Evaluation}
We report mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), coefficient
of determination ($R^2$), and Spearman rank correlation $\rho$.  The constant
baseline predicts the training-target mean.  Mean and sample standard deviation
are computed over the five seeds (11, 22, 33, 44, 55), treating the seed rather
than an individual correlated scenario as the independent replicate.

\subsection{OpenStreetMap transfer}
On September 1, 2026, OSMnx~\cite{boeing2017} was used to download the drivable
network within 650~m of $(10.7626,106.6822)$, near the University of Science,
VNU-HCM.  The directed multigraph was simplified, converted to an undirected
simple graph, and restricted to its largest connected component.  The result
has 163 nodes and 221 edges.  Conductance is $100/\text{length}_{\rm m}$ and
coordinates are min--max normalized.  For each seed, 300 independent random
edge-disruption scenarios are generated.  No OSM scenario is used for training,
early stopping, or model selection: this is zero-shot transfer.  Map data are
credited to OpenStreetMap contributors under the Open Database License.

The ablation increases MAE by only $0.0038$ on average and has essentially no
effect on rank correlation.  The node-level Fiedler coordinate is therefore not
a dominant direct-GCN input.  Residual learning reduces MAE by 40.6\% and RMSE
by 27.5\% relative to direct GCN, while increasing $\rho$ by 0.249.  The
standalone spectral approximation retains the best synthetic ranking but has
much larger squared error.

On OSM transfer, direct GCN reaches $0.1097\pm0.0191$ MAE and
$0.8639\pm0.0223$ rank correlation.  Removing Fiedler changes MAE by only
$-0.0012$ on average.  Residual learning improves MAE to
$0.1018\pm0.0155$ and rank correlation to $0.9269\pm0.0119$.  In contrast,
the standalone first-order approximation deteriorates to $0.5662\pm0.0350$
MAE and negative $R^2$.  The sparse OSM topology lies outside the regime where
a finite multi-edge deletion is well represented by a first-order sum; learning
a correction recovers much of its ranking information.

\section{Discussion}

The experiment answers a narrower question than real road resilience.  It
measures structural connectivity, not travel demand, capacity, delay, or
recovery time.  Moreover, random edge deletion is not a flood model.  Flooded
links are spatially correlated and depend on elevation, drainage, rainfall, and
road geometry.  Accordingly, the present results must be described as
``disruption scenarios'', not flood predictions.

The evaluation exposes a useful methodological lesson.  Randomly splitting
all scenarios would allow damaged variants of one base graph in both training
and test sets, producing optimistic estimates.  Graph-level splitting should
be mandatory in this setting.  A direct GCN uses local message passing and
struggles to rank a global eigenvalue response.  Equation~\eqref{eq:residual}
combines the high-ranking spectral prior with a nonlinear correction, producing
the most balanced method.  Its OSM success is zero-shot, but it is based on one
small geographic area and should not be interpreted as city-wide validation.

\section{Limitations and next experiments}

The following extensions remain necessary:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item add spatially clustered failures and test out-of-range graph sizes;
  \item measure inference time under a fixed batching and hardware protocol;
  \item repeat OSM transfer over several areas and network densities;
  \item only after obtaining a licensed hazard layer, study flood-conditioned
  failure probabilities.
\end{enumerate}
No hypothesis test is emphasized with only five independent seeds.  The current
effect sizes and standard deviations are more informative than treating 1,500
dependent test scenarios as independent replicates.

\section{Conclusion}

Residual spectral--GCN substantially improves direct GCN on synthetic graphs
and provides the best balance of error and ranking under zero-shot OSM transfer.
The Fiedler-feature ablation shows that this gain comes from the explicit
analytical prior rather than merely exposing a spectral node feature.  The
standalone first-order approximation is strong in-distribution but fails on the
sparser OSM network, making the hybrid correction scientifically meaningful.
Claims about urban flooding or policy must still wait for hazard data and
multi-area validation.

\section*{Reproducibility statement}
The complete generators, spectral baseline, three learned configurations,
graph-disjoint split, cached OSM GraphML, aggregation scripts, and generated
tables are provided with the project.  The runs used Python 3.13.11, CPU-only
PyTorch with one compute thread, 2,000 synthetic and 300 OSM scenarios per seed,
at most 100 epochs, and fixed seeds 11, 22, 33, 44, and 55.
